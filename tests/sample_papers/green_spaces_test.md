# The Impact of Urban Green Spaces on Mental Health: A Review and Preliminary Analysis

## Abstract

Urban green spaces have become an increasingly popular topic among planners, psychologists, and public health researchers alike, and honestly, it's not hard to see why. This paper takes a look at how access to parks, gardens, and other vegetated areas within cities relates to mental health outcomes like stress, anxiety, and mood. We pulled together existing literature and ran a small survey-based study (n=142) in a mid-sized U.S. city to see whether self-reported park visitation correlated with lower perceived stress scores. Turns out, it mostly did — folks who visited green spaces at least twice a week reported meaningfully lower stress than those who rarely did, though the effect wasn't huge. We also found some interesting wrinkles, like the fact that quality of green space mattered more than raw quantity in a lot of cases. Overall, the findings line up with a growing body of work suggesting cities ought to prioritize accessible, well-maintained natural areas — not just as a nice-to-have amenity, but as a genuine public health intervention.

## Introduction

Urbanization has increased dramatically over the past several decades, and it is important to note that this trend shows no signs of slowing down. As of recent estimates, more than half of the global population resides in urban areas, and this figure is projected to rise substantially in the coming decades. Consequently, the design and composition of urban environments plays a crucial role in shaping the health and wellbeing of city residents. Furthermore, mental health has emerged as a pressing public health concern worldwide, with rates of anxiety, depression, and chronic stress continuing to increase across many populations.

In light of these developments, researchers have increasingly turned their attention to the built environment as a potential lever for improving population mental health. Urban green spaces, which include parks, community gardens, greenways, and other vegetated public areas, have been proposed as one such lever. It is widely believed that exposure to natural environments confers a range of psychological benefits, including reduced stress, improved mood, and enhanced cognitive functioning. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects, as well as the magnitude of their impact, remain subjects of ongoing investigation. This paper aims to synthesize existing literature on the relationship between urban green space and mental health, and furthermore, to present preliminary empirical findings from a small-scale survey study. In conclusion, it is hoped that this work will contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how urban design decisions influence psychological wellbeing.

## Literature Review

A substantial body of research has explored the psychological benefits associated with exposure to natural environments. It is important to note that this line of inquiry draws on several theoretical frameworks, most notably Attention Restoration Theory and Stress Reduction Theory, both of which posit that natural settings possess unique restorative properties. Attention Restoration Theory, as articulated by Kaplan (1995), suggests that natural environments facilitate the recovery of directed attention capacities that become depleted through sustained cognitive effort in urban and workplace settings. Furthermore, this theoretical perspective has been applied extensively in subsequent studies examining green space and cognitive performance.

Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity, and it is within this broader conception of health that the role of environmental factors, including access to nature, must be understood. Researchers examining urban wellbeing have increasingly adopted this expansive definition, arguing that mental health cannot be meaningfully assessed in isolation from the physical and social conditions in which individuals live. This holistic framing has informed much of the contemporary literature on green space and psychological functioning, including work by Mitchell and Popham (2008), who examined health inequalities across environmental exposure gradients.

In a related vein, some scholars have argued that human beings possess an innate, evolutionarily rooted tendency to seek connection with the natural world, and that urban environments deprived of such connection may contribute to elevated psychological distress. This idea — that humans carry a deep-seated biological pull toward other living things and natural systems, a kind of built-in affinity shaped by our evolutionary history — has been influential in shaping subsequent environmental psychology research, including studies on hospital design and recovery outcomes, such as the well-known work by Ulrich (1984) demonstrating that surgical patients with views of nature recovered more quickly than those without such views.

Additional empirical work has extended these theoretical foundations into applied urban contexts. Kuo and Sullivan (2001) found that vegetation levels in urban housing developments were associated with lower rates of reported crime and aggression, suggesting broader community-level benefits of green infrastructure. Similarly, Bratman, Hamilton, and Daily (2012) reviewed evidence linking nature exposure to improvements in mood, cognitive function, and physiological stress markers. More recently, Whitfield and Osei (2021) reported findings from a multi-city panel study indicating that chromatic properties of vegetation—that is, the specific color qualities of urban plantings—exert measurable effects on resident affect regulation, a finding that, if replicated, would add an intriguing dimension to existing models. Louv (2008), in his influential account of what he termed "nature-deficit disorder," argued that children in particular are increasingly disconnected from natural environments, with potential long-term consequences for psychological development.

## Methodology

For this part of the project, I wanted to keep things fairly simple and manageable given time constraints. We recruited 142 adult participants from a mid-sized U.S. city (population ~310,000) via an online survey distributed through local community boards and social media groups. Participants were asked to report their frequency of visiting urban green spaces over the past month, along with basic demographic information, and they completed the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), a widely used and validated 10-item measure.

We also asked participants to rate the "quality" of their nearest green space on a 5-point scale, considering things like cleanliness, safety, and how much vegetation was actually present (as opposed to, say, a mostly-paved plaza with a couple of potted plants technically counting as "green space" in city records — which, frankly, happens more than you'd think). Data collection ran for about three weeks in spring. Analysis was pretty straightforward: we ran correlations between visitation frequency, perceived quality, and PSS-10 scores, controlling for age and income where possible. It's worth being upfront that this wasn't a randomized or longitudinal design, so causal claims really can't be made here — this is exploratory, correlational work, and should be read that way.

## Results

Participants who reported visiting green spaces two or more times per week had noticeably lower average PSS-10 scores (M = 14.2) compared to those visiting less than once a week (M = 18.6), a difference that was statistically significant (p < .01). Interestingly, though, the correlation between raw visitation frequency and stress scores was moderate at best (r = -.28), while perceived green space quality showed a somewhat stronger relationship (r = -.41). In other words, it wasn't just about going to a park — it mattered whether the park was actually nice.

Income and age were both weakly associated with stress scores but didn't fully explain away the green space effects, which is encouraging from a "this isn't just a proxy for something else" standpoint. About 22% of participants reported that their nearest green space was, in their words, "not somewhere I feel comfortable spending time," which is a notable finding on its own and points to equity issues that deserve more attention than we could give them here.

## Discussion

So what do we make of all this? Honestly, the results here are pretty consistent with what a lot of the existing literature suggests — green space seems to matter for mental health, but quality and accessibility might matter more than sheer presence. That quality mattered more than frequency was, for me, the most interesting takeaway, and it raises some real questions for city planners who might be tempted to check a box by adding minimal, low-maintenance green infrastructure rather than investing in spaces people actually want to use.

There are obvious limitations here. The sample size is small, the design is cross-sectional, and self-report measures always come with some risk of bias (people who are already less stressed might just be more inclined to go outside, rather than the other way around). Still, taken together with prior work, the pattern does seem fairly robust. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs, and frankly, from paying more attention to the equity dimension — the finding that nearly a quarter of participants didn't feel safe or comfortable in their nearest green space is, in my opinion, something that deserves its own dedicated study.

## Conclusion

Taken as a whole, the evidence reviewed and generated in this paper supports the notion that urban green spaces play a meaningful role in supporting mental health, though the relationship is more nuanced than a simple "more parks equals less stress" framing would suggest. Quality, safety, and accessibility appear to condition how much benefit residents actually derive from nearby nature. As cities continue to grow and densify, these findings suggest that thoughtful investment in high-quality, genuinely accessible green infrastructure should be treated as a public health priority rather than a purely aesthetic one.
